A summer in the Biomaterials department, a few days with each of its research groups.

Background

Biomaterials is one of the four departments at the institute. The work starts from structure: how collagen and mineral sit together in bone to make something stiff that does not shatter, and what an arrangement like that is worth to someone designing an implant.

The department splits into small groups, one to a material or a question — bone mechanics, mineralization during development, the micromechanics of shell and tooth, mucus, biofilms, plant fibers — next to Matters of Activity, the cluster that puts designers and architects in with the materials scientists.

I spent a few days with each group. Nobody handed me a project of my own. I prepared samples, ran instruments and worked through the data with whoever owned it, which meant watching the same kind of question get asked in chemistry, in mechanics, in imaging and in design.

Methods

Every rotation came with an instrument, and most of the time went into getting a sample ready for it: sectioned, embedded, stained, coated. What the preparation allowed decided what the measurement could say.

Rotations

Compound eyes in horseshoe crabs

The cornea of a horseshoe crab eye bends light with a refractive index that changes across each ommatidium, a gradient proposed in 1891 that nobody could measure until recently. The material is chitin, laid down either in parallel or in a twist. I ran Raman on hydrated samples, which held more water at the center of an ommatidium than at the edge — part of where the gradient comes from. Horseshoe crabs also carry far fewer ommatidia than other arthropods, which is why they get studied.

Mechanobiology of bone

Mechanobiology

Osteocytes sit in their lacunae for decades and reach each other through canaliculae, and the fluid moving through that network is a candidate for how bone registers load. Load it heavily and it builds; unload it, as happens in space, and it thins. I cut mouse bone on a diamond wire saw, best at about 2.8 on the dial, for two studies in the group: one on brittle bone disease, one on how skull plates fuse in infants. The disease is osteogenesis imperfecta, usually a fault in collagen, and four of its 21 types cover most cases.

Mineralization in developing bone

Development of Mineralized Skeletal Materials

I dissected femurs out of chicken embryos fixed in ethanol, and stained whole embryos of different ages with alcian blue and alizarin red to see how far ossification had gone. Bone begins as cartilage and collagen and hardens only once cells start releasing calcium in vesicles, so a series of ages lays out the sequence. Raman put calcium carbonate in the shell and calcium phosphate in the bone, with carbonate still reading at the ends of a bone while the middle had turned to phosphate — the embryo draws its calcium out of the shell. The rest of the rotation was cryotome sections, immunostaining of the blood vessel walls, and confocal stacks put together in FIJI.

Micromechanics of mineralized tissue

Micromechanics of Biological and Bio-inspired Materials

Pinna shell is calcite, built as a honeycomb of prisms, and the group had cut the walls away from one prism with an ion beam to see how it behaves alone rather than in the bundle. Press an ordinary material and it bulges at the middle; these push the load out in three directions. I indented shark teeth as well, where inner and outer enamel do different jobs out of the same mineral. The standing rule was to size the probe to the feature, a large tip for bulk behavior and a small one for a single structure.

Snail adhesive mucus

Mucus-based Materials

Snails make five mucuses: an adhesive that turns crystalline as it sets, a calcium-rich one for getting through winter, a lubricant, and two for protection. All of them are ions, peptides and protein in some proportion, which is what makes the composition worth chasing. I prepared adhesive mucus for HPLC — 0.1 mg into 500 µL of buffer, ten minutes in a 95 °C dry bath, twenty in the sonicator, three rounds, then the centrifuge. The fraction collector caught the peaks as they came off, protein first and peptides after. SEM mapping gave the elements, molybdenum and aluminum included, which came from the holder and not the sample.

Bacterial biofilms

Biofilm-based Materials

Bacteria grown on agar at different nutrient concentrations build films with different properties, and the group works back from the properties to the proteins behind them. I broke the cells open mechanically and with buffer, heat and sonication, spun the samples down and ran the supernatant out on SDS-PAGE. I cast those gels myself: a lower and an upper layer at different acrylamide concentration and pH, with isopropanol over the lower one to flatten it, so that a sample runs fast and then slows enough to separate. Concentrations came off a Bradford assay against serial dilutions of albumin, fit to a line in Excel and plotted in Prism.

Beetle-killed timber

Matters of Activity

Bark beetles burrow into Bavarian pine to lay their eggs and bring a fungus in with them that blocks water from reaching the crown, and timber from a tree killed that way is graded down. What the fungus does to the structure was the question. I soaked samples, cut them on a microtome along all three orientations, and found it under the microscope as stained lines, cell walls broken down and the cells next to them filled with brown. The designers in the cluster were working on joints: cross lamination cut to lock together instead of glued, so the wood comes apart again, and one group building with reclaimed timber that kept its old nails and took no new ones.

Pine bark as a formable material

Adaptive Fibrous Materials

Bark went the other way, toward what it can be made to do. I tested ways to get it to fold — water, glycerol, freeze and thaw, an iron, an oven — pressing folded samples in a mold and timing every treatment. It holds a fold, opens again in water, and sometimes closes back on its own as it dries. Tannins come out of it only in ionized water. With the treatments that worked I folded a box from a printed pattern and cut a slit to close it.

Aerogels

Hierarchical Structure of Biological and Bio-inspired Materials

A silica gel dried at room pressure shrinks hard while the hexane in its pores evaporates, then springs part of the way back as air moves in. I followed one through a 14-hour micro-CT series and it ended smaller than it started. What is left is 90 to 99 percent air, among the best insulators there is, and blue from Rayleigh scattering.

Takeaways

What stuck was sizing a measurement to the thing being measured. Choose a probe or a section without thinking about the scale of the structure and the instrument still hands back a clean number that means nothing.

I also spent a day with the people who write the analysis code. They were blunt about it: you have to understand the experiment you are coding for, build it in pieces small enough to test as you go, and comment it for whoever inherits it.
