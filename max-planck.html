Potsdam, Germany

Max Planck Institute of Colloids and Interfaces

Jun – Aug 2023

In the summer before my senior year of high school, I worked as a research intern in the Department of Biomaterials, spending several days with each of its research groups.

Biomaterials is one of the four departments at the institute. Their work starts from structure: how collagen and mineral combine in bone to form a material that is stiff yet resists fracture, and how such arrangements can inform the design of implants.

The department is organized into small groups, each focused on a material or a question: bone mechanics, mineralization during development, the micromechanics of shell and tooth, mucus, biofilms, and plant fibers.

Rather than completing a single project, I rotated through the groups, preparing samples, operating instruments, and analyzing data alongside the researchers who led each study. The researchers in each group also explained the work they had completed so far and the direction they planned to take it. The rotations showed how the same structural questions are approached through chemistry, mechanics, imaging, and design.

Most of my time went into sample preparation, including sectioning, embedding, staining, and coating. The quality of the preparation determined what each measurement could reveal.

Rotations

Compound eyes in horseshoe crabs

The cornea of the horseshoe crab focuses light through a refractive index gradient across each ommatidium, a mechanism first proposed in 1891 but only recently measured. The cornea is made of chitin, arranged in either parallel or twisted layers. Using Raman spectroscopy on hydrated samples, I found higher water content at the center of each ommatidium than at its edge, which contributes to the gradient. Horseshoe crabs have far fewer ommatidia than most arthropods, which makes their eyes a useful model for study.

Mechanobiology of bone

Mechanobiology

Osteocytes reside in lacunae for decades and connect to one another through canaliculi. Fluid flow through this network is one proposed mechanism by which bone senses mechanical load. Bone under heavy load gains mass, while unloaded bone, as in spaceflight, loses it. I cut mouse bone on a diamond wire saw for two of the group’s studies, one on osteogenesis imperfecta and one on the fusion of cranial sutures in infants. Osteogenesis imperfecta, or brittle bone disease, usually results from defects in collagen, and four of its 21 known types account for most cases.

Mineralization in developing bone

Development of Mineralized Skeletal Materials

I dissected femurs from ethanol-fixed chicken embryos and stained whole embryos of different ages with alcian blue and alizarin red to track the progress of ossification. Bone forms first as cartilage and collagen and mineralizes only once cells release calcium in vesicles, so embryos of increasing age show the sequence of development. Raman spectroscopy identified calcium carbonate in the eggshell and calcium phosphate in the bone. Carbonate persisted at the ends of the bone after the midshaft had converted to phosphate, consistent with the embryo drawing calcium from the shell. I also cut cryotome sections, immunostained blood vessel walls, and assembled confocal image stacks in FIJI.

Micromechanics of mineralized tissue

Micromechanics of Biological and Bio-inspired Materials

Pinna shell consists of calcite prisms arranged in a honeycomb. The group had used a focused ion beam to remove the walls around a single prism so that its behavior could be tested in isolation. Under compression, an ordinary material bulges at its midsection, whereas these prisms distribute load in three directions. I also performed nanoindentation on shark teeth, in which the inner and outer enamel serve different mechanical functions despite sharing the same mineral. The group’s guiding principle was to match the probe to the feature, using a large tip for bulk properties and a small tip for individual structures.

Snail adhesive mucus

Mucus-based Materials

Snails produce five types of mucus: an adhesive that crystallizes as it sets, a calcium-rich mucus used to survive winter, a lubricant, and two protective mucuses. Each is a mixture of ions, peptides, and proteins in different proportions, and the group aims to relate composition to function. I prepared adhesive mucus for HPLC by dissolving 0.1 mg in 500 µL of buffer, heating it in a 95 °C dry bath for 10 minutes, and sonicating it for 20 minutes, repeating the cycle three times before centrifugation. A fraction collector separated the peaks as they eluted, proteins first and peptides after. SEM elemental mapping also detected molybdenum and aluminum, which came from the sample holder rather than the mucus.

Bacterial biofilms

Biofilm-based Materials

Bacteria grown on agar with different nutrient concentrations form biofilms with different properties, and the group works backward from those properties to the proteins responsible. I lysed cells mechanically and with buffer, heat, and sonication, centrifuged the lysates, and ran the supernatant on SDS-PAGE. I cast the gels myself, layering a stacking gel over a resolving gel of different acrylamide concentration and pH, with isopropanol over the resolving gel to level its surface. This arrangement concentrates each sample before the resolving gel separates its proteins by size. I measured protein concentrations with a Bradford assay against serial dilutions of albumin, fit the standard curve in Excel, and plotted the results in Prism.

Beetle-killed timber

Matters of Activity

Bark beetles that bore into Bavarian pine to lay eggs introduce a fungus that blocks water transport to the crown, and timber from trees killed this way is downgraded. The group studied how the fungus alters the structure of the wood. I soaked samples, sectioned them on a microtome in all three orientations, and identified the damage under the microscope as stained lines, degraded cell walls, and brown deposits in neighboring cells. Designers in the cluster were developing cross-laminated joints that interlock without glue so that the wood can later be disassembled, and one group was building with reclaimed timber that retained its original nails.

Pine bark as a formable material

Adaptive Fibrous Materials

This group studied pine bark as a formable material. I tested treatments to make it foldable, including water, glycerol, freeze–thaw cycles, an iron, and an oven, pressing folded samples in a mold and timing each treatment. Treated bark held a fold, opened when wetted, and sometimes refolded on its own as it dried. Tannins leached from the bark only in ionized water. Using the most effective treatments, I folded a box from a printed template and cut a slit to close it.

Aerogels

Hierarchical Structure of Biological and Bio-inspired Materials

When a silica gel dries at ambient pressure, it shrinks as the hexane in its pores evaporates and then partially recovers as air enters, a process called spring-back. I followed one sample through a 14-hour micro-CT series and found that it ended smaller than it began. The resulting aerogel is 90 to 99 percent air, which makes it one of the most effective thermal insulators known, and it appears blue because of Rayleigh scattering.

What I took away

The internship gave me hands-on experience with a wide range of laboratory techniques. For sample preparation, I sectioned embedded tissue and wood on a microtome and frozen bone on a cryotome, performed hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunostaining, and cast polyacrylamide gels. For characterization, I used Raman spectroscopy to identify chemical bonds and map mineral distribution, X-ray CT and micro-CT to image three-dimensional structure, SEM, TEM, and FIB-SEM for surface and internal imaging and elemental mapping, and nanoindentation to measure the mechanical response of enamel and shell. For biochemical analysis, I separated mucus proteins and peptides by HPLC and quantified biofilm proteins with SDS-PAGE and a Bradford assay.

The most important lesson was to match a measurement to the scale of the structure being measured. A probe or section chosen without regard to scale still produces a clean number, but that number has no meaning.

I also spent a day with the researchers who write the department’s analysis code. They emphasized understanding the experiment before writing code for it, building the code in small pieces that can be tested along the way, and commenting it for future users.
